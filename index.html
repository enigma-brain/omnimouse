OmniMouse: Scaling properties of multi-modal Brain Models

Konstantin Willeke*, Polina Turishcheva*, Alex Gilbert *, et al.

Stanford University, University of Göttingen

Scaling data and models has transformed AI. Does the same hold for brain modeling? We train multi-modal, multi-task models on 3.1 million neurons from 73 mice (150B+ neural tokens), flexibly supporting neural prediction, behavioral decoding, and neural forecasting. OmniMouse achieves state-of-the-art performance, outperforming specialized baselines across virtually all regimes. Yet performance scales with more data while gains from larger models saturate – inverting the standard AI scaling story: brain models remain data-limited even with vast recordings.

Motivation

Scaling data and neural networks has transformed AI: more data and more parameters reliably yield better performance in language and vision. Whether the same principle applies to modeling brain activity is an open question — and one with very different constraints, because neural recordings are costly, comparatively small, and heterogeneous across labs.

The mouse visual cortex is a natural testbed: large-scale datasets exist, standardized benchmarks are in place, and the circuitry is comparatively well-characterized. Yet most brain models target a single modality, task, or session, so the field has intuitions about scaling but little direct evidence of how data size, model size, and task coverage interact at realistic scales.

OmniMouse is a multi-modal, multi-task transformer that unifies neural prediction, behavioral decoding, and forecasting in a single architecture, trained on one of the largest single-neuron corpora to date. Flexible masking lets the same model handle arbitrary combinations of these tasks, which makes it possible to ask, for the first time at this scale, how performance depends jointly on data, model, and task structure.

The OmniMouse Model

OmniMouse processes three time-aligned streams simultaneously: single-neuron calcium traces from visual cortex, the video stimulus shown to the mouse, and five behavioral variables (pupil x/y, pupil size and its derivative, running speed). Each input chunk carries a masking configuration that specifies which samples are visible to the model and which are reconstruction targets, so training and evaluation share the same interface.

Each neuron is tokenized individually via a strided 1D convolution along time, with learned identity embeddings for its neuron, session, and animal; per-neuron embeddings have fixed dimension and a shared projection, so per-neuron parameter count is decoupled from model size. Video frames are encoded with the first ten layers of a Hiera vision transformer, and behavior is provided either as one compact input token per channel or masked and predicted.

The architecture follows an encode–fuse–decode design. A cross-attention encoder with learned latent queries compresses unmasked neural and behavioral tokens into a fixed-length latent sequence, using local sliding-window attention plus a few global "register" tokens. A fusion stack then integrates these latents with video features through transformer layers that interleave sliding-window and global self-attention at a 5:1 ratio. All attention blocks use 1D RoPE derived from each token's timestamp, and a shared cross-attention decoder reads out neuronal and behavioral targets from the fused representation.

The key design choice is flexible masking. We train with 119 structured configurations varying causal context, population context, and stimulus/behavior availability. The same model therefore handles forecasting, stimulus-conditioned prediction, sub-population prediction, and behavioral decoding without task-specific heads — and at evaluation time we can probe mask combinations the model has never seen, turning the viewer below into a small experiment rather than a replay.

Dataset

We compiled one of the largest single-neuron datasets to date: more than 3 million neurons from the visual cortex of 73 mice across 323 sessions, totaling over 150 billion neural tokens. Mice viewed naturalistic movies (cinematic clips, Sports-1M), ImageNet images, and parametric stimuli (Gabors, random dot kinematograms, pink noise, model-generated stimuli) while running on a wheel. Pupil position, pupil dilation and its derivative, and running speed were recorded throughout every session.

For training we reconstruct the full experimental timeline, including blank periods between stimulus blocks, so the model sees a continuous stream rather than isolated trials. Behavior is downsampled to 20 Hz, stimuli to 30 Hz, and calcium traces are linearly upsampled to 30 Hz to match SENSORIUM 2023; token counts refer to samples before upsampling, so the headline dataset size isn't inflated by interpolation.

Results

State-of-the-art performance. OmniMouse outperforms strong specialized baselines across virtually all evaluation regimes — neural forecasting, population prediction, stimulus-conditioned encoding, and behavioral decoding — and sets a new state of the art on the SENSORIUM 2022 and 2023 competitions. These advantages hold in strict data-matched comparisons, where every model sees identical sessions: the unified tokenization, flexible masking, and multi-task training give a consistent edge independent of data scale. The only task where a specialized baseline stays ahead in the data-matched regime is decoding of running speed.

Model scaling saturates. We trained OmniMouse at five sizes — 1M, 5M, 20M, 80M, and 300M — on the full 323-session corpus, tracking test loss and single-trial correlation against model FLOPs for each of five response masking strategies. Performance improved steadily up to ~80M parameters and then plateaued: scaling from 80M to 300M produced essentially no gain, and this held in both data-matched and full-data regimes. Despite a near-4× increase in capacity, brain models at this data scale are not bottlenecked by parameters or compute — the architecture already has enough room for the signal the recordings carry.

Figure 5. Task-specific performance gains with model scaling. Top: mask per task. Middle/bottom: test loss and single-trial correlation as a function of compute. Gains level off once models pass ~80M parameters.

Behavior decoding scales the most. Among our tasks, behavioral decoding showed the cleanest scaling dynamics. Decoding of gaze, pupil dilation, and running speed improved smoothly along both axes, with larger models and larger datasets each yielding consistent gains — reminiscent of the power-law behavior familiar from language and vision. Neither axis saturated at the scales we tested, and the behavioral objective had not fully converged even for the 300M model. Behavior carries a richer and more structured signal than single-neuron prediction, and the same architecture trained on more data at larger scale would likely continue to improve beyond the range we explored.

Figure 6. Behavior decoding scales with model size. Per-variable performance for gaze, pupil size, and running speed continues to improve across the full compute range we tested, with no clear saturation.

Data scaling works. Scaling the dataset tells the opposite story. We trained 5M, 20M, and 80M models on nested collections of 8, 16, 32, 64, and 323 sessions — larger collections strict supersets of smaller ones, all evaluated on the same held-out test set. Across every task and every model size, additional sessions produced monotonic gains with no sign of saturation. The gains are disproportionately larger for bigger models: the gap between small and large networks widens as the dataset grows — the signature of a data-limited rather than capacity-limited system.

Figure 7. Data scaling. Nested training collections from 8 to 323 sessions. Performance improves monotonically for every task and every model size, and larger models pull further ahead as the dataset grows.

An inverted scaling story. Together, these results invert the standard AI narrative. In language and vision, massive datasets made parameter scaling the primary driver of progress. In brain modeling — even for the mouse visual cortex — models remain data-limited despite vast recordings. The bottleneck is not compute; it is data: more diverse stimuli, more varied tasks, more animals, richer behavior.

Conclusions

OmniMouse shows that a single unified model can match or exceed strong specialized methods across neural prediction, forecasting, and behavioral decoding — trained on one of the largest single-neuron corpora to date. Its breadth makes it possible to ask, for the first time at this scale, how much remaining headroom comes from better architectures versus simply more and richer data.

The scaling analysis offers a clear answer: with current data, brain models are not compute- or capacity-limited. Performance scales reliably with dataset size, but gains from model size saturate around 80M for neural prediction. Even the relatively simple mouse visual system is still hungry for more diverse stimuli, richer behavior, and larger-scale recordings.

If brain models are to become foundation models for neuroscience, the field must invest in richer experiments — more stimuli, more tasks, more behavioral paradigms, more animals and areas. In language modeling, small improvements from scaling eventually unlocked phase transitions to qualitatively new capabilities; by analogy, richer neuroscience data may unlock new capabilities in brain models and reveal deeper principles of neural computation. OmniMouse's unified, maskable design is one step toward that regime.
